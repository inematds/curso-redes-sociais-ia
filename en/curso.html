Choose a promise you can keep

You can define your audience, usefulness, and profile limits.

Trying to speak to everyone makes it harder to choose what to post next. A specific promise guides what belongs and what does not.

In 1 minute

  1. Audience: people with small desks
  2. Usefulness: organize with available materials
  3. Limit: simple examples without unverified claims

1An editorial promise guides your choices

Editorial promise is the benefit your content aims to deliver regularly.

Bia, a 33-year-old creator, helps Nelson, a 58-year-old craftsperson.

They plan Reuso de Bolso, a fictional organization profile using simple materials.

The promise is to share small ideas and explain their limits.

Write one line: I help people with small desks organize materials using items they already own.

Set the topic boundaries: desk organization, no electrical repairs or unmeasured environmental claims.

You can work on another topic.

Change the example while keeping the audience, usefulness, and limit.

Bia uses the promise to turn down topics that pull the profile off course.

Open worksheets and comparison · 33 openings · Seven-day planner.

2The course result fits in one folder

You will leave with three pillars, a seven-day calendar, and one original video published.

A pillar is a recurring content category.

In this example: organize, reuse paper, and show behind the scenes.

The calendar includes production and observation; it does not require seven daily posts.

Set aside 10 to 14 hours to study and create, plus the follow-up week.

Use one main platform and an editor you already know.

The course teaches a testing system.

Going viral depends on factors you cannot control and is not the promised outcome.

Nelson chooses examples he can explain with common materials.

Test one difference at a time

You can plan two openings while keeping the rest comparable.

When everything changes between videos, it is hard to know which choice is worth repeating.

In 1 minute

  1. Hypothesis: showing the result first may clarify the idea
  2. Variable: opening; keep the rest similar
  3. Review: same interval and tentative conclusion

1Testing starts with a small hypothesis

The Test, Observe, and Repeat method organizes the next attempt.

A hypothesis is an assumption you will observe.

Example: showing the finished jar may make the idea clearer.

Compare it with an opening that shows scattered pencils.

Keep the topic, duration, and explanation similar.

Write two opening lines.

A: see one way to gather these pencils.

B: does your desk look like this after working?

Both need to deliver the promised organization.

Avoid also changing the voice, music, object, and duration if the main question is the opening.

Bia writes down the hypothesis before looking at numbers.

Open worksheets and comparison · 33 openings · Seven-day planner.

2Comparing posts alone does not prove cause

Reach, timing, and context can differ between posts.

A simple test guides your work; it does not prove the opening caused the whole result.

Define what you will observe and when.

Use the same interval after each post.

For this lesson, plan the versions; you do not need to publish twice.

Save one as a future alternative.

When you find a promising signal, repeat the idea in another relevant post.

Do not turn one observation into a rule about the algorithm.

Nelson saves the alternative version so he does not have to recreate everything from memory.

3Check the decision against its use

Change the main opening and record the limits of the comparison.

Nelson uses the sheet to check: same interval and tentative conclusion.

Test yourself

If you got stuck here, that’s normalIf your hypothesis has three changes, choose one to start with. The others can become later tests.

Read two signals with comparable bases

You can compare simple rates without confusing reach and usefulness.

A higher action count may come only from more views. A comparison must show the base used.

In 1 minute

  1. A: 20 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 2%
  2. B: 15 ÷ 500 × 100 = 3%
  3. Conclusion: B has a higher rate; A has more total actions

1Counts and rates answer different questions

Use two available signals: attention and useful action.

Attention might be average watch time, when reported.

A useful action might be a share or save, depending on your goal.

Record the metric’s exact name and definition on the platform.

Do not mix accounts from different platforms.

Fictional example: A had 1,000 views and 20 shares; B had 500 views and 15 shares.

Shares per view: A is 2%; B is 3%.

A generated more total shares.

B had the higher ratio in this sample.

No number proves sales or the preferences of everyone.

Bia records the metric definition before comparing.

Open worksheets and comparison · 33 openings · Seven-day planner.

2The interval must match the base

Open the workbook’s comparison chart and check the calculation: actions divided by views, times one hundred.

Use values from the same interval.

A zero base makes the rate undefined.

A missing metric should stay unavailable, not become zero.

On Shorts, views and engaged views have different definitions.

Since March 2025, the general view count includes starts and replays with no minimum watch time.

Choose a metric and keep its definition consistent.

For this practice, use the fictional example; add your real data after publishing.

Nelson keeps the value unknown when the dashboard does not provide it.

3Check the decision against its use

Use compatible metrics, bases, and intervals, and identify the source.

Nelson uses the sheet to check: conclusion: B has a higher rate; A has more total actions.

Test yourself

A has 20 actions per 1,000 views; B has 15 per 500. Which reading is correct?

If you got stuck here, that’s normalYou do not need to master statistics to start. Record what was counted, which base was used, and when you observed it.

Turn an idea into a readable carousel

You can plan three pages with a beginning, explanation, and ending.

Splitting one long sentence across many pages does not create a useful sequence. Each page needs a purpose.

In 1 minute

  1. Page 1: visible problem and short title
  2. Page 2: matching solution
  3. Page 3: care tip and next step

1A carousel is a sequence that needs continuity

In this case, three pages are enough for an exercise: problem, solution, and care tip.

The first shows scattered pencils.

The second shows the jar gathering the pencils.

The third reminds viewers to use a sturdy, stable container.

The sequence does not claim to physically test a generated image.

Write a short title for each page.

Use: scattered pencils; gather them in a stable container; check the rim before use.

Leave room for reading.

A real demonstration photo should match what you did.

Label an AI illustration as a visual example.

Bia checks that each page adds new information.

Show useful information from the first page.
